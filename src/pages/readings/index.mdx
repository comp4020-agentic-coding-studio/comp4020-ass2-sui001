# Frameworks and readings

The weekly Tutes and lectures name these frameworks in passing,
assuming you already have a working sense of them by the time they come up
a second or third time. This page is where that sense comes from: a
paragraph per idea, and the source if you want to read further than a
paragraph. If you're looking for how to actually log an observation of one
of these in the wild, that's the [log template](/log-template/) instead.

## Civil inattention {#civil-inattention}

Erving Goffman's term for the specific, minimal courtesy strangers extend
each other in public: a glance that registers "I see you, you're a person,
I'm not a threat," immediately followed by looking away and granting the
other person the right to pass unbothered. It isn't the same as ignoring
someone; true ignoring is a snub. Civil inattention is closer to a
handshake that both parties perform automatically and neither remembers
doing.

> Goffman, E. (1963). *Behavior in Public Places: Notes on the Social
> Organization of Gatherings*. Free Press.

## The interaction order {#interaction-order}

Goffman's broader claim: that face-to-face conduct between people who
share a physical space runs on its own stable set of rules, distinct from
whatever institutional or legal rules also apply. Coexistence 101 mostly
lives here: the interaction order is what a shared kitchen, an open-plan
office and a bus queue all have in common, even though almost nothing else
about them is the same.

> Goffman, E. (1983). The interaction order. *American Sociological
> Review*, 48(1), 1–17.

## Tie-signs and remedial interchanges {#tie-signs}

Two smaller Goffman terms worth having a name for. A **tie-sign** is a
public display that announces the nature of a relationship without saying
it in words: walking arm in arm, sitting closer than a stranger would.
A **remedial interchange** is the small ritual that repairs a minor breach
of shared-space etiquette after it happens: "sorry," a nod, stepping back.
Watch for both once you know to look.

> Goffman, E. (1971). *Relations in Public: Microstudies of the Public
> Order*. Basic Books.

## Proxemics {#proxemics}

Edward Hall's framework for interpersonal distance, split into four zones
(intimate, personal, social and public), each associated with a
different kind of relationship and a different register of voice and
touch. Which zone two people default to is rarely a conscious choice, and
watching it is one of the fastest ways to read a room's actual social
structure rather than its stated one.

> Hall, E. T. (1966). *The Hidden Dimension*. Doubleday.

## Third place {#third-place}

Ray Oldenburg's name for a category of space that is neither home (the
"first place") nor work (the "second place"): low stakes, no fixed rank,
and a habit of showing up that isn't tied to any obligation. A café with
regulars, a community garden, the same bench at the same time most days.
Oldenburg argued that a person or a society without enough third places
loses something civic life depends on; this course treats that as a
testable claim about a specific space, not a nostalgic one.

> Oldenburg, R. (1989). *The Great Good Place: Cafes, Coffee Shops,
> Bookstores, Bars, Hair Salons, and Other Hangouts at the Heart of a
> Community*. Paragon House.

## Personal and social capability {#personal-and-social-capability}

The Australian Curriculum's existing general capability covering
self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and social management
at a school level. Coexistence 101 covers overlapping ground but stays
hands-on and field-based rather than staying at the level of a capability
statement; see Week 10 for where the two frameworks actually diverge.

> [Australian Curriculum: Personal and Social Capability](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-general-capability/personal-and-social-capability)
